# How accurate is gcdatlas?

## The short answer

**No, it is not a carbon copy of the universe, and it should not be described as one.** It is a scientifically grounded model: the *where* and *how big* are real measurements, the *what it looks like* is a physically based artist's rendering, and time is sped up.

Suggested wording for the site or for sharing:

> Every named object is at its real position and true size, taken from star catalogues and published measurements. How each one looks is a physically based rendering in ASCII: brightness is compressed so faint things stay visible, and fast processes are sped up.

## What is real, what is approximate, what is artistic

| Topic | Status | Details |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positions of ~2,000 bright stars | **Real** | Hipparcos catalogue positions and parallax distances. Parallax errors grow with distance: a few percent within 100 light-years, 10–30% beyond several hundred. |
| Star colours and sizes | **Real (rounded)** | Colour from measured temperature or colour index; radii for named stars from the literature. Many giant-star radii are uncertain by 10–50% (Betelgeuse, UY Scuti, Stephenson 2-18 especially). |
| Planet positions | **Real, approximate** | JPL approximate Keplerian elements: arcminute-level from 1800 to 2050, degrading outside. Moon: a low-precision lunar theory (a fraction of a degree). |
| Planet appearance | **Mixed** | Earth, near enough (0.11.0): its land and sea are NASA's Blue Marble Next Generation of the month the atlas shows (2004, a cloud-free mosaic with topography and bathymetry, about 10 km a pixel on a desk), its relief lit by the Sun from GEBCO's heights raised 7 times (illustrative), its coast Natural Earth's 10 m land, its night lights NASA's Black Marble 2016 (about 10 km a pixel on a desk). Up close (0.12.0) the land is four times sharper: Blue Marble's 500 m images of January and July laid as detail over the month's map, about 2.4 km a pixel. Near the ground anywhere its heights stand up in 3D from GEBCO 2008 at about 2 km a pixel, so they are smoother than the real mountains; the clouds there are illustrative (a clear day). From far away, and before the maps load, a painted map on real coastlines (Natural Earth) and ice sheets. Clouds (0.13.0) are NASA GIBS's true-colour VIIRS picture of a recent day (a snapshot a day or two old, each place as seen at about 1:30 pm; they do not move), with snow and ice told apart from cloud by the month's Blue Marble; near the ground the amount of cloud comes from that picture, its shapes and heights are illustrative; without the picture (offline) the clouds are made up. Weather events are illustrative and sped up: tropical cyclones appear only in the ocean basins active in the current month and move along made-up tracks (their eyes are drawn a little wider than life so they show as characters); thunderstorm clusters and lightning sit over the real stormiest regions (Congo basin, Lake Maracaibo, the Amazon, Borneo, and seasonal ones); Saharan dust and fire seasons follow the months they really happen, but the individual storms, flashes and fires are made up. Other planets are procedural textures in their real colours and banding. |
| Satellites | **Real (live) or representative** | With live data: every active satellite from CelesTrak, propagated with two-body motion plus J2 (a few km over hours; full SGP4 is not used). Without it: representative orbital shells. The ISS and Hubble use the live elements when available. |
| Air traffic | **Simulated** | Aircraft on 55 real busy routes, timed from great-circle distances; not live flights. |
| Rocket launches | **Real schedule, modelled flights** | Next launches and pads from Launch Library 2. SpaceX flights (Starship, Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, Crew Dragon) are drawn at their scheduled time from the real pad in real time, but the path, the timings and the events are one typical flight of each kind modelled on real ones (Starship Flight 5, a Starlink Falcon 9, a Falcon Heavy with its side boosters landing at Landing Zones 1 and 2 as on its first flight in 2018 (those zones have since been retired), a Crew Dragon flight from SLC-40, where all Dragons now launch), not that day's trajectory; a scrubbed launch may still be drawn until the schedule updates (at most an hour). Clicked flights and replays are sped up in the quiet parts (the caption says replay; the readout says so). Seen from orbit, the exhaust trail is drawn up to 10 times wider than life so it shows. Launch azimuths, droneship positions (465 to 585 km downrange) and the landing zones are typical. The pads, towers, droneships and vehicles are simplified models at their real sizes. Clicked flights and replays are moved to the nearest afternoon at the pad (the atlas clock eases there), so they are lit by day; live launches keep the real time. Near the ground the sky hides everything beyond the Solar System (illustrative: real air hides faint galaxies by day, and thins with height). The sound (0.10.0) is synthesized, not recorded: its timing (the speed of sound, 343 m/s), its fall with distance and its absence in near-vacuum follow physics; its loudness is scaled for listening. The camera shake, the ground cloud's size, the shock ring at ignition and the vapour cone are illustrative (the vapour cone is shown on every flight near Mach 1; real flights show it only in damp air). The smoke column follows the flight's path and drifts with the real wind; its width and how long it lasts are illustrative. Dragon waits docked at the ISS's forward port when no flight is on (a Dragon is often there, not always), and its docking "a day later" moves the atlas clock on about a day, to a moment when the station is in sunlight. |
| The ground round the launch sites | **Real images and heights, of different dates** | Starbase, Cape Canaveral with Kennedy, and Vandenberg (`tools/earth-detail.mjs`). Round the pads, aerial photos: at Starbase NOAA NGS coastal photos of 18 January 2026 (0.3 m a pixel, 84% of the 6.4 km layer and all of the 1.6 km one), with USDA NAIP of 14 October 2024 (0.6 m) where NOAA did not fly, its colours shifted to match NOAA's round each patch (the pixels are the 2024 photo's; only their tone is changed); NOAA's camera looks down at 37.5 degrees, so tall structures lean (the towers show as long streaks across the ground, like shadows) while the ground is in place. At Cape Canaveral USGS NAIP, 0.3 m a pixel, flown 9 January 2023; Vandenberg has no NAIP photo there (the base is masked) and uses Sentinel-2. Regions 410 km and 51 km across: Copernicus Sentinel-2 L2A true colour (10 m a pixel), the clearest scenes from 2025 and 2026, up to four a tile, so neighbouring tiles can be from different days (their seams over the open sea are hidden by drawing it one colour). Colours: the photos as they are; the satellite images brightened to match them over the same land. Positions: the images' own (a few metres). Heights: AWS Terrain Tiles (USGS 3DEP in the United States, a few metres), plus about 2,300 OpenStreetMap buildings, from their height or levels tags where mapped, else a typical height for their kind (hangars 14 m, offices 12 m, sheds 3 m): many heights are guesses, and buildings newer than the map are missing; underground structures and lattice towers are left out. Within 150 m of each pad our own models stand instead. Water is wherever the terrain is at sea level. |
| The famous places (0.15.0) | **Real images and heights, an illustrative sky** | 30 places (`tools/earth-places.mjs`), each in two to four square layers from 410 km down to 12.8 km across (3.2 or 6.4 km at Giza, Hoover Dam, the Great Wall and the Three Gorges Dam), 1024 pixels each: from about 400 m a pixel down to about 12.5 m (3 m at Giza and Hoover Dam). Colours: Copernicus Sentinel-2 L2A true colour, cloud-free composites of the clearest scenes of a chosen season of 2024 to 2026 (the snow of the Himalaya and the Alps kept in its tones, the Okavango in flood), so a layer mixes several days; at the Grand Canyon and Hoover Dam the finest layer is a USGS NAIP aerial photo. Heights: Copernicus DEM GLO-30 (30 m, from radar flown 2011 to 2015, so it has the dams, islands and canals built before then; peaks come out tens of metres low: the Matterhorn's finest layer tops out at 4,325 m against 4,478 m), or USGS 3DEP in the United States, stored in steps of 1 to 34 m; the relief is not raised. Added from OpenStreetMap: the tall buildings round the Palm (40 m or more, from their height tags or 3.4 m a level), the Great Wall's line at Mutianyu (a ribbon 8 m wide and 7 m high, illustrative), and the three pyramids of Giza as simple models on their OpenStreetMap footprints (Khufu 138.5 m, Khafre 136.4 m, Menkaure 61 m, their present heights). The Palm lies within Dubai, so near it the city's own layers are drawn (0.13.0). Water is wherever the heights are at sea level or Sentinel-2 sees water, in the images' own colours (so reefs and lagoons show; elsewhere near the ground the open sea is drawn in one colour). Near the ground the sky over a famous place is a clear day with a few wisps of high cloud (illustrative: the real clouds of the day hid several of them), and each is flown to at a moment when the Sun is about 32 degrees up and lights its first view from the side (the clock moves there, as for a launch). Its facts were checked against NPS, USGS, UNESCO, ESA, swisstopo, the Smithsonian and the papers named in `tools/earth-places.mjs`; Mont Blanc's height changes with its snow cap. |
| Weather and clouds over the launch sites | **Real weather, drawn clouds** | Open-Meteo's hourly forecast and recent past (`/api/weather`, three days back to two ahead) for the three sites: cloud cover low, mid and high, wind at 10 m and 850 hPa (about 1.5 km), visibility, rain and humidity, at the hour nearest the atlas clock. The cover, the wind that carries the clouds and the smoke, the haze and the rain are real; the clouds' shapes are drawn (noise), their heights typical (low clouds from the humidity, about 25 m for every point below 100%; altocumulus at 4.5 km; cirrus at 9 km). The clouds drift at the flight's pace (faster in its sped-up parts). A camera inside the low layer sees clear air round itself (a camera plane flies between clouds). Offline: a fair day, a few clouds (illustrative). |
| Tesla Roadster | **Real position, illustrative spin** | JPL Horizons state vectors (solution 11) every 40 days from 2018 to 2069, joined by cubic curves: within about 32,000 km of Horizons' daily positions in 2026 and 160,000 km at the 2047 pass by Earth. After 2069 it coasts on a two-body orbit. The close passes are Horizons' list. The model (car, Starman, adapter, upper stage) follows photos. The car has the 2008 Roadster's published size (3.95 m long, 1.85 m wide, 2.35 m between the axles) and its real wheel and tyre sizes; its shapes are simplified, and its tilt on the adapter (16 degrees, nose up) is judged from SpaceX's photos. Its slow spin, kept tilted toward the Sun so the car is lit, is illustrative, as nobody has measured its spin since 2018. Some fill light from above, and its soft reflection in the paint and the windscreen, are added so the car reads in characters; in space the shaded side would be nearly black. |
| Nebulae | **Real position and size, artistic look** | Procedural volumes shaped after Hubble/JWST images (Pillars, Orion, Eagle, Helix…). Colours follow the familiar image palettes, which are themselves false-colour composites; the Pillars follow the JWST NIRCam palette. Their moving gas (evaporating veils, shimmering edges) is real in kind but sped up enormously: the real flows take thousands of years to change. |
| Galaxies | **Real position, distance, size and tilt; artistic structure** | Spiral arms, bars, dust lanes and star clouds are parametric models. The Milky Way's arm layout follows current radio and Gaia models, which are still debated. |
| Large-scale universe | **Illustrative** | The cosmic web and Laniakea show the right scale and character, not the real mapped filaments. |
| Black holes | **Physics-based** | Light paths are integrated through the Schwarzschild metric (lensing, shadow at 2.6 r_s, photon ring). Disks use a thin-disk temperature profile with Doppler beaming and gravitational redshift. Gaia BH1's and Gaia BH3's companions are lensed with their glow widened (as stars are drawn elsewhere) so each one's bent image spans a character; Cygnus X-1's outer disk is a particle cloud whose points are sized to the patch of disk they stand for. Simplifications: non-spinning holes, simplified disk structure. Time-dilation readouts are exact for a static observer. |
| Binary stars, tidal disruption | **Simplified physics** | RS Ophiuchi's and T Coronae Borealis's gas streams follow the restricted three-body equations; their novae are replays every 34 seconds (T CrB last erupted in 1946). The star torn apart uses the "frozen-in" approximation, a star drawn about 10x too large, and a simplified circularisation of the debris. |
| Mergers and gravitational waves | **Illustrative** | Real masses and frequencies in the readouts; the spacetime ripples are exaggerated by an enormous factor to be visible. |
| Time | **Sped up** | The Solar System clock defaults to 10 minutes per second; explosions, orbits and mergers are compressed. Deep-time star drift uses straight-line proper motions (valid for roughly ±100,000 years). |
| Brightness | **Compressed** | Real brightness spans 30+ orders of magnitude; the renderer tone-maps it so both a quasar and a comet are visible. Some tiny sources (a quasar's engine, a kilonova) are drawn magnified, and the ruler shows their true size. |
| Earth's story | **Broad strokes** | Dates are standard textbook values (rounded). The globe shows each era's broad look (molten, ocean world, snowball, bare rock, green), not the ancient continents. |
| Sun glare from the planets | **Illustrative** | The Sun is drawn at its true size seen from each planet; the soft round glow and halo around it are a camera-style glare so a disc that small reads as blinding. |
| Betelgeuse surface | **Illustrative** | A few giant convection cells as in the ALMA and VLT images; the fine granules and filaments seen close up are simulated, not observed. The dusty plumes drifting outward follow the VLT/VISIR images in spirit; their shapes and speed are illustrative. |
| Solar System overview | **Enlarged bodies, true order** | In the whole-system view the Sun and planets are drawn enlarged so they are visible. The Sun is sized first as a share of the screen (4% of the screen height at the widest view, growing gently to 10% at the closest, never smaller than its true size); each planet is drawn at Sun size x (true radius ratio)^0.3, so the Sun is always largest and the planets keep their true order of size, but the ratios are compressed (really the Sun is 10x Jupiter, Jupiter 11x Earth; the readout says so). Planets whose orbit falls inside the enlarged Sun are hidden and named in the readout. The Sun's corona is faded while enlarged. Positions and orbits are to scale. Close up, everything is true size. In free camera the overview only switches on for a wide view with no planet near the camera, so a planet never swells around you. |
| Star surfaces | **Physically inspired** | Stars cooler than about 7,000 K have convective surfaces; they are drawn with churning granulation and flame-like spicules at the limb, much faster than real (granules last about 10 minutes). |
| The Sun's colour | **Artistic tint** | Drawn warm yellow-orange with boiling, pulsing granulation, the way filtered solar photos look. Its real light (5,772 K) is white when seen from space; the atmosphere makes it look yellow from the ground. The readout says so. |
| New worlds (0.7.5) | **Real orbits, sped-up years** | Comets and small bodies use JPL small-body elements; exoplanet orbits keep their real sizes but their periods are shortened so you can see them move (the readout gives the real period). Planet surfaces and 'Oumuamua's shape are illustrative. Tabby's Star's dust clouds are drawn where they could be; nobody has seen them. |
| Famous comets (0.8.1) | **Real orbits, a frozen moment, illustrative look** | Hale-Bopp, NEOWISE, Tsuchinshan-ATLAS, 3I/ATLAS and 67P sit on their real orbits (JPL Horizons osculating elements for that date) at the moment named in the readout (perihelion, or closest to Earth), not where they are today; the readout gives today's distance from the Sun. They only appear while you visit them, so the Solar System view still shows today's sky. Their tails point away from the Sun and the dust tails bend back along the orbit, as real ones do, but the lengths, widths, brightness and the coma's shells are illustrative. 67P's two lobes use Rosetta's measured sizes; the other nuclei's shapes are made up and their sizes are the published estimates (Tsuchinshan-ATLAS's nucleus is unmeasured, at most about 12 km across: it is drawn 5 km across). The jets are illustrative. Nuclei are drawn much brighter than their real 4 to 6% reflectivity. |
| Shoemaker-Levy 9 (0.8.1) | **Replay** | Real impact latitude (44°S), speed (60 km/s), plume height (about 3,000 km) and scar size (about Earth's). The fragments follow one illustrative path onto one spot just past the limb, and six days of impacts are compressed into about 20 seconds, in a loop. The fragments' sizes are illustrative (G, K and L the largest, as observed). |
| Kreutz sungrazers (0.8.1) | **Replay** | Real parabolic motion on orbits scattered around the Great Comet of 1843's (perihelion 1.2 Sun radii from the centre), 15,000 times faster than real. How many comets there are, their tails and which one survives the passage are illustrative. |
| Meteor showers (0.8.1) | **Real orbits, illustrative dust** | The Perseid and Leonid streams follow the real orbits of Comets Swift-Tuttle and Tempel-Tuttle (JPL elements), crossing Earth's path where the showers happen; the date countdown follows Earth's real position. How the dust is spread across and along the orbit is illustrative, and the grains near Earth's crossing flow about 200,000 times faster than they really move. The parent comet is shown where it is now. |
| Comets and meteors on tour trips | **Illustrative** | About one tour trip in three a comet or meteor streak passes the camera. It is decoration drawn in the camera's own frame, not a real object, and it never changes the flight. Inside the Solar System it is a comet whose tails point away from the Sun; elsewhere a meteor-like streak (real meteors only glow in a planet's air). |
| Voyager 1 (0.8.3) | **Real path, straight-line model** | It moves with the Solar System clock along a straight line through its JPL Horizons position and velocity for 18 November 2026. Since its 1980 Saturn flyby it has barely curved: the line stays within about 0.02 AU of Horizons from 2020 to 2030 and within 1 AU back to 2000. Before 1990 it is drawn where it was in 1990, and its readout says so. The one-light-day countdown uses this path and Earth's real position; it gives 18 November 2026, the date NASA announced. |
| TRAPPIST-1e's surface (0.8.3) | **Illustrative** | Its size and orbit are measured. It is drawn as a temperate world that keeps one side to its star (expected for a planet this close, not observed): open sea under the star, a few rocky islands, ice beyond and across the night side, clouds over the warmest water and a thin blue haze of air. Nobody knows yet whether it has air or water; its readout says so. |
| Places added in 0.9.0 | **Real positions, sizes and orbits; drawn looks** | Vesta and Bennu follow JPL Horizons orbits (osculating elements for 2026-09-27) and IAU/JPL poles and spins. Vesta is drawn as its measured ellipsoid (573 x 557 x 446 km) with Rheasilvia (centred at 72 deg S, 86 deg E, as in the IAU gazetteer), its central peak and the equatorial troughs at true height, but simplified: not the real shape model. Its shading falls off more steeply than real and its slopes are shaded 2.5 times steeper than real, so the low relief shows in characters; the shadows its rim and peak cast use the true heights. Bennu's size is measured (505 x 492 x 457 m); its boulders are illustrative and it is drawn much brighter than its real 4% reflectivity. Mimas's Herschel follows NASA's figures (130 km wide, walls about 5 km, floor about 10 km deep, peak 6 km) with a simplified profile; its floor is drawn a shade darker, and its slopes are shaded 2.5 times steeper than real so the walls and peak still show in characters with the Sun high over them (the shadows they cast use the true heights). Saturn's moons have their real orbits and periods, but where Mimas, Enceladus and Titan are along their orbits is not their real place (Tethys, Dione and Rhea's are, since 0.23.0): so the times Mimas passes through Saturn's shadow are not the real ones either (the length of each pass and the years it happens in are real). The planets of other stars have sped-up orbits (the readout gives the real period); K2-18 b and HD 189733 b pass in front of their stars, so their orbits are drawn at their measured tilt, almost edge-on from Earth; 51 Pegasi b's width has never been measured (drawn Jupiter-sized) and its colours and K2-18 b's are guesses; HD 189733 b's deep blue was measured by Hubble, its bands are drawn. Gaia BH3's orbit is sped up. The Lagoon Nebula's look is artistic (its size, its dark lane, the Hourglass and its cluster are real), and the Great Hercules Cluster is drawn as 22,000 points and a glow standing for several hundred thousand stars. |
| Messier objects added in 0.16.0 | **Real positions, distances, sizes and tilts; drawn looks** | Positions are SIMBAD's. Distances: Gaia DR3 parallaxes of the central stars for the Dumbbell and the Owl (Bailer-Jones et al. 2021; popular sources still give 1,360 and 2,030 light-years), Gaia distances of their young stars for the Trifid and the Omega (Kuhn et al. 2019; a maser parallax puts the Omega at about 6,500 light-years instead of 5,500), Gaia open-cluster distances (Hunt & Reffert 2023), Baumgardt & Vasiliev 2021 for the globular clusters, and for the galaxies Cosmicflows-4 (tip of the red giant branch, surface brightness fluctuations), PHANGS, M106's masers (Reid et al. 2019) and Cepheids for M77 (2026; older estimates say up to 47 million light-years, and its readout says so). Sizes come from each object's size on the sky (HyperLeda's D25 for galaxies) at that distance. Galaxies and nebulae are turned to their real position angle on the sky and galaxies tilted to their measured inclination; which side of a disc is nearer is not known, so that is a guess. The looks are drawn: the Dumbbell's hourglass and the Owl's tilted hollow follow published models in shape only; the Trifid's three dark lanes, its blue reflection nebula and the Omega's bar and hook sit roughly where photos show them; the globular clusters' stars are made up in number and colour mix to suit their age (the open clusters' stars are real since 0.18.0, below); globular clusters, like M13, are 12,000 to 20,000 points standing for hundreds of thousands of stars. NGC 3628 is drawn beside M65 and M66 but not listed (it is on neither checklist). M84 and M86 sit at their own measured distances, 4.6 million light-years apart in depth (within the errors), so Markarian's Chain lines up only as seen from Earth. |
| Atlas distances (0.9.0) | **Real, measured from two places** | Under the Solar System and Comets & meteors headings the atlas gives each place's distance from the Sun (a comet's where it is today, although it is drawn at its famous moment) and a moon's distance from its planet, so the column climbs outward in the planets' order. Every other heading, the one-list sorts and the info card give distances from Earth. So Venus reads about 0.7 AU in the atlas and between 0.3 and 1.7 AU from Earth on its card: both are true, and each heading says which it is. Distances are measured each time the atlas opens, so bodies on eccentric orbits (Bennu, Pluto, the comets) can change places in the list over the years. Sorted by size, the atlas gives each place's true size across; the observable universe is 93 billion light-years, and so is the cosmic web, which fills it (the drawn part of the web is a model about 4 billion light-years across). |
| Planet and moon surfaces (0.9.4) | **Real places, strengthened contrast** | The markings are soft patches placed from published maps at their real latitudes and longitudes (Mars's dark markings, Hellas, Olympus Mons and Valles Marineris; the Moon's seas and rayed craters; Mercury's Caloris; Pluto's heart); small craters are random. Colours are close to true colour, but bright and dark markings differ more than they really do, the light is kept nearly level across the lit side, and relief is shaded with slopes steeper than real, so they read as characters. Venus's cloud patterns are the ones ultraviolet photos show; to the eye it is nearly plain, and its readout says so. Mars shows both polar caps at once at a spring size; the real caps grow and shrink with its seasons. Neptune is drawn a little bluer than its true pale azure (Irwin et al. 2024) so it can be told from Uranus, and its dark spot is one of many that come and go (Voyager 2's 1989 spot had gone by 1994). Uranus's bright polar hood is as seen in the 2020s. The Great Red Spot is drawn about 16,000 km wide (about 40,000 km in the 1880s, about 14,000 km now), not at its real longitude. Saturn's small white storms are generic; its big ones come about every 30 years. |
| The Halo, its shield and Pip | **Fiction** | The only invented objects are the Halo and Pip, its little drone, and everything they do is invented too: its light-speed jumps and folds through space (the hull burning away into embers and forming again, a starburst at each end), its shield, scans, Pip's outings round the ship, weapons tests and skims. Weapons tests are purely visual: nothing about the body changes and every blast fades completely. Its path and speed are cinematic, scaled to the body it visits, not physical: it roams round each place for about two minutes in passes and U-turn loops (about a minute on the Halo tour, whose stops are real places from the atlas's tours), near a planet at a few percent to a third of light speed (the size of the place sets the pace). What is real: it flies round the real bodies at their true positions and sizes, its scan's hologram lies on each body's drawn surface with brackets where the atlas draws each feature, the numbers the scan finds are real, and the pull its shield works against is real. The shield's power and the escape speed in the readout come from the real escape speed where the ship is: sqrt(r_s/r) of light speed near a black hole (18 to 19% at the closest point of a pass, 27 to 31 times r_s out), a body's surface escape speed x sqrt(R/r) elsewhere (NASA fact sheets; stars from their mass and radius, Betelgeuse and Antares only roughly). SGR 1806-20's mass is not measured, so it is taken as 1.4 Suns, a typical neutron star, and its readout says "about ... (for a typical 1.4-Sun neutron star)" instead of "(real)". That far out, a ship moving that fast would not be captured, so the readout says it holds course in the hole's gravity or climbs out of it, never that it is pulled in. Pip stays within about 3 ship radii (8 km) of the ship (the far end when it flies beside the camera). The railgun's craters glow and cool from white to red and dark within 7 s and leave nothing; shots at a black hole fall in and fade at the edge of its shadow (the curve they take is drawn for the look, not worked out). Its attack runs and signature moves are flown round real features where the atlas draws them: through Saturn's Cassini Division as the atlas draws it, 1.95 to 2.03 Saturn radii out (the real division is 117,580 to 122,170 km from Saturn's middle, 2.02 to 2.10 radii, so the drawn gap sits a little inside it); low over the Great Red Spot, Valles Marineris, Tycho and Pele where their shaders put them (about 85 km over Mars and 35 km over the Moon); over Earth about as high as the space station (1.064 Earth radii, about 410 km); through a star's corona at 1.25 of its radius; and inside galaxies and nebulae along arcs and weaves drawn for the look. Near a black hole or a magnetar it keeps within about 1 ship radius and moves more gently, and its readout gives no reason for that: at 27 to 31 times r_s the drone would not be captured either, so it is a choice of the story, not physics. The scan's look and timing are invented; its numbers come from NASA NSSDCA fact sheets (diameters, equatorial gravity, sidereal rotation; Io, Europa and Titan gravity from GM/R²), Jupiter's System III, Saturn about 10 h 34 min (Mankovich et al. 2019, Cassini ring seismology), Ceres (Dawn, Park et al. 2016), Halley (Giotto, Keller et al. 1986), the Sun (NASA Sun fact sheet, a Carrington period of about 25 days; IAU 5,772 K), the stars as the atlas already gives them (Kervella et al. 2017 and 2003, Bond et al. 2017, Agol et al. 2021, Mann et al. 2019, Ohnaka et al. 2013, Joyce et al. 2020), SGR 1806-20's spin (Kouveliotou et al. 1998) with the size of a typical neutron star, the Antennae's distance as a range (Saviane et al. 2008 give 43 million light-years, Schweizer et al. 2008 give 73 million), the LMC's width from NASA, and the black holes, nebulae and galaxies from their own readouts in the atlas. Saturn is drawn at its mean radius; the scan gives its equatorial width. |

| Music | **Fiction** | Space is silent. The songs are made up live in your browser; their names borrow places in the atlas and say nothing about them. |
| Messier objects added in 0.18.0, and every open cluster | **Real stars for the open clusters; real positions and distances; drawn nebulae** | Every open cluster (the 16 of 0.18.0 and the Beehive, the Wild Duck and Ptolemy's Cluster of 0.16.0) is drawn from its brightest Gaia DR3 members, up to 600, as Hunt & Reffert 2023 list them: each star at its real place on the sky, with its real G brightness and its real BP-RP colour, less the reddening of the cluster (E(BP-RP) taken as 0.42 times its AV) and turned into a temperature by a formula for dwarf stars (Mucciarelli & Bellazzini 2020), so giants come out a little warm. Only the depth of each star along our line of sight is made up: a bell curve as wide as the cluster on the sky, because one star's parallax is not precise enough at these distances. The fainter members, beyond the 600, are left out. Distances are Hunt & Reffert's; ages are from Cantat-Gaudin et al. 2020 and Dias et al. 2021, given as a range where they disagree (the catalogue's own ages are left out: they put M67 at 1.7 billion years against the published 4). A cluster's size in its readout is how far its drawn stars spread (twice the radius holding nine in ten of them), not the smaller patch seen in a telescope. M76's distance is statistical (about 3,400 light-years, uncertain by about 40%: Gaia has no usable parallax for its central star); its ring and lobes are drawn after the published model (a ring seen nearly edge-on), not mapped. M78 is drawn as dust lit by its two stars, mostly blue; its lanes and shape are rough. M24's stars lie 10,000 to 16,000 light-years away; the cloud is drawn at the near end and its depth is illustrative, as are its speckle and dust; Barnard 92 and 93 sit roughly where photos show them. NGC 6603 is drawn from its Gaia members but not listed. |
| Globular clusters added in 0.21.0, Pease 1 and the Local Group dwarfs | **Real positions, distances, sizes and shapes; stars made up to match** | The 25 globular clusters of 0.21.0 sit at SIMBAD's positions and Baumgardt & Vasiliev 2021's distances; their size across is SEDS's size on the sky at that distance. Pease 1 is at the Gaia position of its central star, at M15's distance; its shell and faint halo are drawn at their measured sizes (Alves, Bond & Livio 2000), its shape is drawn. The eight dwarf galaxies are at SIMBAD's positions and their newest measured distances, turned to their measured position angle and ellipticity (McConnachie 2012 and its 2021 table); a dwarf irregular's tilt comes from its axis ratio. Their stars, clumps and glow are made up to suit their kind; the irregulars' half-light radii disagree by up to four times between papers, so IC 1613's and WLM's are drawn tighter than McConnachie's. |
| Tethys, Dione, Rhea, Capella and Spica (0.23.0) | **Real places, orbits and features; drawn looks** | Tethys, Dione and Rhea are at their real places along their orbits: mean longitudes fitted to JPL's ephemeris (SAT441, through Horizons; within 0.3 deg from 1980 to 2050), with Tethys's swing of 2 deg either way from its resonance with Mimas, so they pass through Saturn's shadow at the real times; each turns as the IAU's rotation model has it. ## Rules for keeping it honest

1. Anything illustrative, magnified, simulated or sped up says so in its readout or fact.
2. Numbers in facts and readouts must match a citable source; round rather than invent precision.
3. When a measurement is disputed (distances to red supergiants, the Milky Way's arms), say so in the fact.
4. Update this table whenever something new is added that is not measured data.
